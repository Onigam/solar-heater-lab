## 0. Summary and the plan against the bar

### 0.1 The machine

**A 300-litre closed-circuit roof thermosiphon: two flat collectors of 1.87 m² aperture each, directly below a horizontal tank 510 mm across, on one frame. This is the Solahart 302J as its maker's August 2023 data sheet draws it (S6): 2.43 m wide, 2.49 m along the roof, 196 kg empty, 515 kg full.** The sun heats a mix of water and propylene glycol in 70 steel risers. The warm mix climbs with no pump into a jacket of about 10 litres wrapped round the drinking-water vessel, gives up its heat, and sinks back down an outside pipe. An electric booster in the tank covers cloudy days.

Why this one and not another (§8, Q1): it is the only thermosiphon for which I could read, together, a maker's dimensioned drawing, a 60-page manual with the full installation order (S7), an independent collector certificate and an independent system certificate (S8, S9), and **Solar Keymark annual results at four European sites**, Athens, Würzburg, Davos and Stockholm (S7 p. 33). Its size and layout are also those the science studied: the Lawrence Berkeley model has 2 × 1.95 m² and a Ø508 mm, 302-litre tank (S17), the Cyprus rigs 3 m² at 45° (S18). The lab draws a **reconstruction of that machine**: no logo in the 3D, the maker named only in the Sources panel.

### 0.2 What the check of the sources changed

1. **Neither video in the roadmap shows a thermosiphon.** The Low-tech Lab's tutorial (S2, S4) is a **pumped closed loop**: a circulator starts when the panel is 10 °C above the tank and stops under 5 °C, with a regulator and an exchanger tank. No word of a tank above the collector. K1000's part 2 (S3) films a preheating buffer tank fed by well water and never says how the heat arrives; part 1 could not be reached. The Low-tech wiki has a second page of nearly the same name (S5), made of plastic bottles, which is a thermosiphon, gives no figure and says it has not been tested in a temperate climate. So the machine, the numbers and the film's question come from other sources (§1, proposed main video S1).
2. **The « aha » of the roadmap is true, with a precise condition and a modest size.** The one source that sweeps the tank's height (S17, a model, 1981): reverse flow is negligible while **the bottom of the tank is level with or above the top of the collector**, and substantial below. By eye from its figure 9: 10 % of the day's gain lost by morning with the tank 0.61 m too low, 21 % at 1.22 m (K28). A measured rig in Cyprus, with tank bottoms 25 to 240 mm above the collector (derived, K31), calls reverse flow « rather unimportant ». The honest story is: a well-placed tank loses about 2 kWh a night anyway, through its own skin; a badly placed one roughly doubles that. **I found no measurement of a flat-plate rig with the tank below the collector's top: every figure in that zone is a 1981 model that asks for its own validation.** The lab says so once.
3. **The thief is also the guard.** The same trickle of reverse flow is studied as a frost protection for direct collectors (S21, S22), and S18 calls « very minimal reverse flow » desirable in winter. A one-way valve cures the first and removes the second (K33). That is the film's best turn.
4. **The real machine sits right on the threshold.** On the roof its tank rests on spacers at the top of the collectors, so the bottom of the tank is about level with the collector's top (y ≈ 0 ± 5 cm, derived from the side view, K30). The typical Cypriot system is set the same way (S26). The often repeated « at least 18 inches above » is a practitioners' belief, reported as such by a patent (S29), not a finding; and one simulation even puts its annual optimum with the tank 15–20 cm **below** the collector's top (S26), though it never mentions the night. **So the threshold is soft near zero.** Where the loss starts to matter on our machine is for the model to find.
5. **It is not water that climbs in this machine.** It is a propylene-glycol mix; the drinking water never leaves the tank. The film's question « How does hot water rise with no pump? » is exact for a direct system and needs one honest sentence here (Q2). The simulation offers both circuits.
6. **The thermal core needs four things before it can run this lab** (§5.1): `liquidWater()` has a constant density, so it gives **zero buoyancy**; its fluid-crossing helpers assume « a fluid holds far less heat than a mass », which is false at night, when 19 litres take three hours to turn over at 6 L/h; and `solarGain` and the loop's head are still to be written.
7. **The maker's own numbers differ with the date.** 2013 manual: 495 kg full, 2.31 m wide, 2.475 m long. 2023 sheet: 515 kg, 2.43 m, 2.49 m. I take the 2023 sheet. The manual's South African table and one of its Keymark rows hold checkable slips (§2.9).
8. **No maker publishes the flow, the riser bore, the pipe bore or the jacket's exchange coefficient.** The single thing that makes this machine a thermosiphon is the one thing nobody measures on it. Flow figures here come from a model (S17), a simulation checked against a rig (S20) and one dye test on another kind of collector (S23). Each stays flagged (§2.10).
9. **A stale statistic circulates.** « 75 % of the world's solar thermal and 90 % of new installations are thermosiphons » is still printed in 2020 (S19's introduction, from Weiss et al. 2019). IEA SHC's 2025 edition says about 53 % of systems installed, and 24 % of new systems in China (K47). Use 53 %.

**The test stand, for `lab-modeler`.** At y = 0 the tank is exactly where the maker's drawing puts it, on its spacers, and the stand is hidden. Moved away from 0 it appears, translucent: the tank goes up on it, or down **beside** the collector bank where it would otherwise collide (a real layout, S29), and the two pipes re-route and stretch. It carries no word; the height shows in the panel. `house` always shows y = 0.

**A lab with no moving part.** Nothing turns here: that is the point of the machine and the modeler's challenge. What moves: the dots in the pipes (true speed about 12 cm/s in the pipes by day and under 2 cm/s backward at night, derived, K37), the colour of the layers, the sun and its shadows, the shower. A film can hold on this: the dots run in real time.

### 0.5 The two guides

The modes stay (the frame is locked); both are filled from the maker's real order, and I say where that order ends.

- **Assembly (20): how it is built, then how it goes up.** Steps 1–8 are the factory: **our reconstruction** from the material list (S6, S7 p. 29), no source gives that order. Steps 9–20 are the **maker's order on the roof, reported** (S7 p. 18–20), down to the details that make a good guide: the collectors stay in their carton, the rail is raised 12 mm per collector on one side, the cold pipe goes on before the hot, the tank is filled with water before the closed circuit.
- **Installation (10): how it joins the house.** Reported where the manual speaks (S7 p. 10–16), said generally where it is the country's rule. The order of the valves on the cold line is the maker's.
- **Honest, and said once at the top of each guide:** this is a reading of a maker's manual for learning, not a procedure. Every step ends on its origin (_reported_, _usual practice_, _our reconstruction_), as in the sand lab, and a data test can check it. No torque, no tool list invented. The manual says only trained installers may fill the closed circuit, and the guide says so at that step.
- **BRIEF §8 applies:** mains pressure, 95 °C water and work on a roof. The maker's figures appear because they are the maker's; there is no « build your own » dimension. The Low-tech panels and the home builds are named once, in a lesson, with what they are.
- **The mechanics still hold:** nothing floats at any step; the test that every id of `rig`, `collector` and `tank-and-tap` is installed in exactly one step applies.

The roadmap's four (plan, collector section, schematic, bill of materials) are in.

**Lessons:** 1 `why-it-climbs` (density, the height–temperature loop, a push of about 4 mm of water, why tall helps and pipes do not) · 2 `black-plate-under-glass` (the efficiency curve, what the sun gives and the air takes, black paint against selective: which wins depends on how hot you run) · 3 `the-layers` (stratification, why the coldest water must reach the collector, a mixed tank against a layered one, the draw from the top) · 4 `the-night` (**the « aha »**: raise and lower the tank, the 2 kWh that go anyway, the 2 more that a bad tank adds, the one-way valve, and the frost guard it removes) · 5 `too-cold-too-hot` (glycol and its table, a burst collector, the holiday week, 109 °C in a closed tank, why a relief valve drips) · 6 `the-shower` (the squeeze between scald and legionella, the mixing valve, the booster's timer, what fraction of your hot water, from Stockholm to Athens). Two quiz questions each.

**Spoken guide:** 51 parts × about 270 characters, three star parts (`water-column`, `hot-pipe`, `riser-tube`) at about 400, four worlds, an introduction: about 20,000 characters per language. Character to follow: **one warm drop** that climbs at a snail's pace and hands its heat through a steel wall, in the Pelton manner, and a cold drop that would like to go the wrong way at night. Anonymous as usual. Said once: reconstruction, model figures, a warning at the shower, one at the roof. Written after the checkpoint, from the final sheets. Local voice (`ROADMAP` §10).

**Sanity checks and conflicts.**

- **Energy balance.** No thermosiphon source gives out more than comes in, except a seller's words: S42's French video says « rendement » up to 95 %, and explains it as absorbing 95 % of the sun. That is the absorptance of a coating (S13's 95/5); certified zero-loss efficiencies are 0.67–0.78 (K25). The same description calls the fluid water and ethanol, and glycol an insulation for the tank. Not used.
- **S35's « 40 K in 3 h ».** 200 L × 40 K in 3 h is 3.1 kW. On the 4 m² his article mentions, that is 775 W/m² delivered, more than a good collector gives at full sun: 3 h of sun cannot raise the **mean** by 40 K. His own warning covers it: an unknown sensor, near the top of a layered tank. Kept as a picture of a real build, not as a figure.
- **S18's « 1.4 GJ a year ».** 7,000 kJ × 365 = 2.56 GJ; 1.4 GJ is 200 nights. Unstated. The measured 7,000 kJ stands, the annual figure is not used. It also cites Morrison as both [6] and [7].
- **S7's table of p. 35.** « Heat loss coefficient » 2.93 W/K for the 180 L and 2.04 W/K for the 300 L, against standing losses of 2.085 and 2.595 kWh a day, which at 45 K give 1.93 and 2.40 W/K. The bigger tank cannot lose less: the coefficients look exchanged or defined otherwise. I use the standing loss, tagged, and range the model from 2 to 3 W/K (ISFH measured 2.21 for 200 L).
- **S7's p. 31, 301KF row.** The QL columns at 300 and 400 L/day repeat Qd (16655 16655). A misprint: not that row.
- **S20's « cloudy conditions ».** Its 35–45 kg/h and 20–30 K make about 1.2 kW, 615 W/m² delivered on 1.89 m². That is no cloud. Probably a lost phrase, or an upper range. The figure stays `compare`, with this note.
- **Pipe insulation.** S7 p. 18 says the hot and cold pipes need none on a tiled roof; S20 ranks pipe insulation over 10 % on annual output. A real disagreement between a maker and a simulation. A switch in the model; `find`.
- **Horizontal tanks.** ADEME calls them « bien moins performants » (S33), S17's stratification effect is 10 %, and all compact thermosiphons use them. The lesson says why they do it anyway.
- **Direct or closed in the cold.** SOLTRAIN and the maker agree. In S2 the question does not arise: it has a pump.
- **Reverse flow's size.** S18, S17 and S23 agree that a well-placed tank loses little by it; S24 finds a daytime effect on an almost flat manifold, which is another geometry. S18 is a Cypriot climate, mild nights.
- **Nothing here is a measurement of our whole machine on a roof.** K21 is an EN 12976 test by ITW and a calculation; K20 is TRNSYS; K66 is measured, on similar systems elsewhere. `compare` tests report, they do not validate.

## 5. Physics, for `lab-physicist`

Pure TypeScript, SI inside, cheap enough for a phone. Dimensions from `dims.ts`. **Never tuned to the `compare` figures.** Start from `~/Dev/sand-battery-lab/src/sim/thermal/` (I read all of it). It says it moves to `@onigam/lab-kit/sim/thermal` when a second lab uses it: this is that lab, or the stove. **The lead decides before the physicist starts** (§9): the BRIEF forbids copying kit code, and the kit's `src/sim` still has only `electric` and `hydraulic`.

### 5.1 What the core gives, and what this lab must add

**Reuse:** `createNetwork`, `stepNetwork`, `stableStep`, `setLinkConductance`, `totalEnthalpy`, `totalBoundaryEnergy`; `seriesU`, `surfaceLoss`, `flowHeat`, `effectiveness`; `tubeFlow`, `tubePressureDrop`; `buoyancyMix`; `makeMaterial`.

**Traps I found reading it:**

1. **`liquidWater()` is `constantMaterial('water', 1000, 4186, 0.6)`.** Constant density gives **no buoyancy at all**. Add ρ(T) and μ(T): Kell (1975) for density and IAPWS for viscosity (S45: read the originals and type the coefficients), then the glycol mix by concentration (S46).
2. **`fluidLine` and `marchFluid`: « The nodes are taken as constant during the crossing: a fluid holds far less heat than a mass. »** True for air in stone, false here. The loop's fluid is the capacity at night: 19 L take 9 minutes to turn over by day and over 3 hours at −6 L/h (K37), and that slow drift **is** the physics. So the loop is a chain of fluid nodes with their own capacity, advected upwind, whichever way the flow runs (LBL's Figure 4 has two branches for this reason). Do not cross them in one step.
3. **`stableStep` knows only conduction links.** Advection passed through `net.Q` is not in it: limit the step to C_i / (|ṁ|·c) yourself.
4. **`tubeFlow(mdot, D, gas)`.** Call it with |ṁ|: `Re = max(ṁD/Aμ, 1)` goes to 1 for a negative flow. `GasProperties` works for a liquid. The jump at Re = 2300 falls where LBL finds the headers (2000–5000): it ran both limits and reports the band.
5. **`buoyancyMix` allocates three arrays per call.** Harmless at 10 layers once a step; a version with a preset workspace is a small gift to the core.
6. **A warning from S17 (p. 25):** a variable time step gave its earlier model **false oscillations all night** with water. Keep the loop's internal step fixed, a few seconds to at most about 10 s of simulated time.

**Add, generic, no lab name, in the core:** `fluids.ts` (water, glycol mix) · `solar.ts` (`solarGain`: ISO 9806 curve with incidence modifier; sun position; plane irradiance; `skyTemperature`) · `loop.ts` (`buoyantHead(z, ρ)`: the signed integral round a closed loop, which is also the stove's `stackDraft`; a flow solver with friction) · in `stratify.ts`: `horizontalCylinderLayers(V, n)` (segment volumes for `w`) and a draw from the top with an inlet mixing parameter.

**Stays in this lab:** the machine's geometry, valves and their logic, the booster, the tempering valve, alarms, scenarios, presets.

### 5.2 The sun and the collector

- **Sun:** declination, hour angle, incidence on the plane; beam and diffuse from a clearness per month that reproduces K50; **air** as a sine on the monthly mean (LBL uses ±5.5 K, keep it as an assumption). A seeded cloud series (the kit's `rng`). The kit has no `sim/climate` yet, so this goes in the core's `solar.ts` or the lab.
- **Collector by day, segments along the flow (4–6 nodes per bank):** absorbed = A·K(θ)·F'(τα)·G, lost = A·F'U_L·(T − Ta) and a quadratic term. **Derive F' from the certificate once** (K39: F'(τα) 0.829, F'U_L 8.34 on gross area, linear form; do the same with K17's second-order form) instead of pasting K17 onto a flow that is not its test flow. That is using a certificate as an input, not tuning.
- **Thermal capacity:** about 6 kJ/m²K (S11, S12's a5: other collectors, estimate) plus the fluid in the nodes.
- **At night, the sky.** The ISO curve knows only the air. Below about 50 W/m², add a sink to the sky: T_sky by Swinbank (T_sky = 0.0552·T_a^1.5, in K; from memory, **read the paper**), ε_eff by a plate–glass–sky chain. Black paint ε about 0.9 (estimate), selective 0.05–0.15 (S13, S20). Gives the plate below the air on clear nights: a `find`, against K33 (below the air all night, no figure), and 6–13 K below from search summaries on other surfaces (s). **The physics role reports the depression; the frost alarm rests on it.**
- **Selective switch:** `black` (K17) or `selective` (S10's curve, and its emissivity). A parameter, no 3D change except the plate's colour.

### 5.3 The loop

Nodes in order, signed by the flow: jacket out → `cold-pipe` (2–3 nodes) → lower header → risers, 70 in parallel (4–6 nodes) → upper header → `hot-pipe` (2 nodes) → jacket (4 nodes: 2 rising, 2 sinking, bottom ports, S27, S20). Each has a height z, a capacity, a loss to air (pipes: a switch between bare and insulated, K45 against S7).

- **Head:** Δp = −g ∮ρ(T) dz round the loop, summed over the nodes. **Plot it**: T against z, closed; the signed area is the push (S17 fig. 11). This chart is the lesson's star and the sim panel's second. A check: with a linear tank and no pipe loss, `buoyantHead` must return S26's eq. 12, hT = ½(Si − So)[2(H3 − H1) − (H2 − H1) − (H3 − H5)²/(H4 − H5)].
- **Friction:** Darcy on each stretch with `tubeFlow`, K factors for bends (LBL: 10 elbows, 4 tees). Mostly laminar: Δp ∝ Q, so Q ∝ head. Both limits at the transition, as S17 does.
- **Flow:** momentum with a relaxation, or quasi-steady with under-relaxation, time constant some tens of seconds. Sign follows the head. **Reversal is not a mode: it is the sign.** A one-way valve is a very large resistance for Q < 0.
- **Jacket to vessel:** a U·A per node to the layer beside it, about 200–400 W/K in all (**estimate**, 150–300 W/m²K on 1.3 m²; S27 has the real correlation, not read; LBL's 170 W/m²K is for tubes). Sensitivity row.
- **Circuit switch:** `closed` (glycol mix, jacket) or `direct` (water, tank bottom to collector, hot return at Hr above the tank's bottom). Viscosity matters: S17 finds lower flow, larger rise, nearly the same energy with glycol. Let the model say it.
- **y:** the only geometric input the slider moves. Pipes' lengths and z follow. Default 0; LBL's range.

### 5.4 The tank and the draw

- **Layers:** 10 in a horizontal cylinder, volumes by circular segment (K6's estimate Ø0.44 × 2.0 m), state = temperature; `buoyancyMix(T, w)` each step, conduction between layers through water and steel (estimate, the wall dominates).
- **Loss:** U·A to air, 2.0–3.0 W/K (K14, K40, §2.9), shared by surface, a little more on top.
- **Draw:** from the top; cold at the bottom, with a mixing parameter (K40: 0.055; the `cold-diffuser` switch; K44 is the check).
- **Booster:** heats the layers above the element (150 L, K6) when the thermostat at its height is under the set point and the mode allows: `off`, `timer`, `always`. Default `off`: the machine as a solar water heater. `always` is the scenario that shows solar fraction collapse.
- **Tempering valve:** T_tap = min(T_top, T_set), share of cold from the balance. T_set 45–50 °C; off is a scenario. A shower: 10 L/min at 40 °C for 8 min (usual figures, **assumption**).
- **Expansion:** tracked: litres out of the cold relief as the mean temperature rises (K60). `state.reliefDrip` drives the 3D's drip.
- **Limits:** combined valve at 99 °C or 1000 kPa; the jacket at 80 kPa; overheat valve closes at a set point not published (**estimate**, 80–90 °C; a parameter). No boiling model: past saturation the fluid is flagged and the alarm speaks; say so in `docs/physics.md`.
- **Frost:** the coldest collector node against the mix's freezing point (K12 for closed; 0 °C for direct, no latent heat). The alarm says « ice forms » and the scenario stops there; bursts are not simulated.

## 7. Safety, law and credits

**Hazards, for the sheets and one warning each, said once where the gesture is risky.**

- **Scald. At the shower.** Summer tanks run at 60–70 °C and may reach 95 °C (K11). Adults burn in 2–5 s at 60 °C, children about four times faster (K53). The maker asks for a tempering valve in every case, rated for 99 °C, and warns to test the water before entering (S7 p. 2, 15). Said once in `tempering-valve` and once in the guide.
- **Legionella. In the same breath: the squeeze.** It grows at 25–45 °C in standing water (K55). A solar tank can sit at those temperatures through a dull spell unless it is boosted, which is why boosters and 60 °C exist (the model will say how long); a preheating buffer tank at 36–38 °C such as S3's sits in that zone (a fact about the temperature, no claim of harm). The lab shows the tank's top in that band as a notice, says what the rules ask (K54, K55), and never says the risk is measured here. Note: 300 L is under France's 400 L threshold for the storage rule (K55), and the 3-litre rule still applies to the pipes.
- **Freezing.** A direct collector can freeze on a clear night **above** 0 °C air (K33: outlet below air; surface can go 6–13 K below in other studies, s). The maker excludes open circuits below 5 °C (K12). Glycol to −7…−34 °C by concentration; below −15 °C, ask (K13). Said once, in lesson 5.
- **Stagnation.** 109 °C in a closed tank, +3.7 bar, measured (K42). Collector 200–220 °C selective (K25), about 119 °C black (K64, weak). Glycol degrades when overheated; I found no peer-reviewed limit, only makers' words at about 150 °C (S44). **Relief discharge is hot**: never block a drain, never let it fall on the roof or in a gutter (S7 p. 14). Do not remove the jacket's valve while it runs (S7 p. 3). Said once, at `tpr-valve`.
- **Roof and weight.** 515 kg full, 0.83 kN/m² averaged, 1.7 kN/m under the tank (K59), and within wind and snow limits (K13). Lifting device; a roof slips; ask a builder or structural engineer (S7 p. 10). Collectors in sunlight are hot: the carton stays on (S7 p. 18). Said once, at `roof-structure`.
- **Drinking water.** Two liquids separated by one steel wall, with a colour that tells: **blue water at the tap means a leak** of food-grade glycol (S7 p. 3). A non-return valve keeps the tank's water out of the street's (S7 p. 27). Rainwater tanks must not catch glycol: it kills their algae and fouls the water (S7 p. 21). A fridge grid has carried refrigerant and oil, and it stays outside a drinking-water loop; none of the sources I read says so outright: **our statement**, kept to one line.
- **Hydrogen.** After two weeks unused, flammable hydrogen may gather in the cylinder; run a sink tap, no flame near (K15). Odd, true, one line in `inner-vessel`'s sheet.
- **Mains electricity.** 240 V, 8–15 A at the element; qualified electrician; power only after filling (S6, S7 p. 3, 20).

**The home-build note, one sentence and a lesson.** Tutorials exist (S4, S5, S35, S42) and may be good. The lab teaches why a pipe that is too narrow stops the climb, and gives no dimensions to copy. The Low-tech Lab's panels get an honest credit: they are pumped.

**Law, per country.** I am not qualified to classify anything, and the visitor reads « the rules of your country apply ». What I could read, or point at:

- **France:** arrêté du 23 juin 1978, article 36 (read, S36): 50 °C and 60 °C at taps, legionella rules.
